\begin{keyidea}{Goals and logical structure}
European claims have one prescribed cash-flow date. An American claim lets
its holder choose a stopping time, so its price combines no-arbitrage
valuation with a timing decision:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{exercise right}&\longrightarrow\text{optimal stopping under }\Q\\
&\longrightarrow\text{backward induction / Snell envelope}\\
&\longrightarrow\text{obstacle PDE and free boundary}\\
&\longrightarrow\text{numerical methods: trees, simulation, finite differences}.
\end{aligned}
\]
At every date ask one question: is immediate exercise worth more than
continuation?
\end{keyidea}

% ============================================================
\section{The exercise right changes the pricing problem}
% ============================================================

\subsection{European and American exercise}

\begin{definition}[European and American claims]
Let \(g_t\) be the cash payoff received if a claim is exercised at date
\(t\). A \emph{European} claim pays \(g_T\) at one fixed maturity \(T\).
An \emph{American} claim may be exercised at any stopping time
\[
\tau\in\mathcal T_{t,T}
=\{\tau:\tau\text{ is a stopping time and }t\leq\tau\leq T\}.
\]
Its cash flow is \(g_\tau\).
\end{definition}

For a stock \(S\) and strike \(K\), the exercise payoffs are
\[
g^{\rm call}(S_t)=(S_t-K)^+,\qquad
g^{\rm put}(S_t)=(K-S_t)^+.
\]
The payoff map is the same for European and American contracts; only the
timing right differs. The word American describes the exercise convention.
It does not describe the stock or the exchange.

\begin{boardexample}{more rights cannot reduce value}
The holder of an American option can choose \(\tau=T\), reproducing the
European exercise policy. Hence
\[
V_t^{\rm Am}\geq V_t^{\rm Eur}.
\]
The difference is the \emph{early-exercise premium}; it can be zero even
though the American holder owns an additional right.
\end{boardexample}

\subsection{The economic trade-off of early exercise}

Exercising a put early means receiving \(K-S_t\) now and investing the cash:
with positive rates, this earns interest on the strike. Waiting keeps the
downside insurance: a further fall in the stock can increase the eventual
payoff. Neither effect dominates everywhere, so an in-the-money option is not
automatically exercised: it should be exercised only when its intrinsic value
is at least its \emph{continuation value}.

For a call on a non-dividend-paying stock, the trade-off is one-sided: early
exercise means paying \(K\) earlier and giving up the optionality, with no
compensating cash flow. Section~\ref{sec:calls} shows that early exercise is
then never optimal. Dividends change this conclusion.

\begin{warningbox}{A stopping time is essential}
The holder cannot select an exercise date after seeing future prices. The
exercise decision at date \(t\) may use only \(\cF_t\): formally,
\(\{\tau=t\}\in\cF_t\) for every \(t\). ``Exercise the put the first time the
stock is below \(80\)'' is admissible; ``exercise today if tomorrow's price
will be below \(80\)'' is not. A future-dependent choice would overstate the
contract value.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{Buyer's value and seller's price}

Let \(B_t=e^{rt}\) be the bank account and \(X^\pi\) the wealth of a
self-financing strategy. The writer does not know which stopping time the
holder will choose, so the writer must be ready whenever the holder exercises.
Thus \(\pi\) is a super-hedge when
\[
X^\pi_u\geq g_u\quad\text{for all }u\in[t,T],
\]
equivalently \(X^\pi_\tau\geq g_\tau\) for every admissible stopping time.

\begin{keyidea}{Two equivalent views in a complete market}
\[
\boxed{
V_t^{\rm Am}
=\inf_{\pi}\{X^\pi_t:X^\pi_u\geq g_u\ \forall u\in[t,T]\}
=\esssup_{\tau\in\mathcal T_{t,T}}
\E^\Q[e^{-r(\tau-t)}g_\tau\mid\cF_t].}
\]
The first formula is the seller's minimal super-hedging capital; the second
is the buyer's optimal-stopping value. Completeness is the bridge between
them: the same number, seen from two sides.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{studentexercise}{first comparison}
Which inequality always holds between American and European options with the
same payoff and maturity? Does it mean that exercise is optimal at time zero?
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
\(V_0^{\rm Am}\geq V_0^{\rm Eur}\), because waiting to maturity is
available. It does not identify the optimal policy: the holder may continue
at every date before maturity, in which case the two values coincide.
\end{shortsolution}

% ============================================================
\section{Discrete time: backward induction}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Risk-neutral recursion}

Consider dates \(0,1,\ldots,N\), a bank account growing by \(R=1+r>0\) per
period, and an equivalent martingale measure \(\Q\). Let \(Y_n=g(S_n)\). The
American value is
\[
V_n=\esssup_{\tau\in\mathcal T_{n,N}}
\E^\Q[R^{-(\tau-n)}Y_\tau\mid\cF_n].
\]
At each date, every feasible future exercise rule is evaluated under \(\Q\)
and the best one is chosen; the rule may adapt to the information revealed
along the path. At maturity there is no remaining choice: \(V_N=Y_N\).

\medskip
\begin{theorem}[Bellman recursion]
\[
\boxed{
V_n=\max\left\{Y_n,\frac1R\E^\Q[V_{n+1}\mid\cF_n]\right\},
\qquad V_N=Y_N.}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Any exercise policy either stops now, giving \(Y_n\), or continues for at
least one period. In the latter case the best remaining value is
\(R^{-1}\E^\Q[V_{n+1}\mid\cF_n]\), because \(V_{n+1}\) already reflects
optimal later choices. Take the better alternative.
\end{proof}

\begin{assumptionbox}{Exercise versus continuation}
Define the exercise value \(E_n=Y_n\) and the continuation value
\[
C_n=\frac1R\E^\Q[V_{n+1}\mid\cF_n].
\]
Then \(E_n>C_n\) means exercise, \(E_n<C_n\) means continue, and equality
means that either action is optimal. The set of states where \(E_n\geq C_n\)
is the \emph{exercise region}; its complement is the \emph{continuation
region}.
\end{assumptionbox}

\subsection{A binomial implementation}

In a binomial model
\[
S_{n+1}=\begin{cases}uS_n,&\text{up},\\dS_n,&\text{down},\end{cases}
\qquad d<R<u,
\]
the risk-neutral up probability is \(p=(R-d)/(u-d)\). Thus
\[
C_n=\frac{pV_{n+1}^{u}+(1-p)V_{n+1}^{d}}{R},
\qquad V_n=\max\{g(S_n),C_n\}.
\]
Work from terminal payoffs backward through the tree, recording nodes where
the intrinsic value wins. The price and the exercise policy are computed
together.

\begin{keyidea}{The decision depends on the node, not on the date}
In the same tree, the holder of an American put may continue at an up node
and exercise at a down node of the same date. The output is an
\emph{exercise region}, not a pre-announced exercise date. The difference
\(V_0^{\rm Am}-V_0^{\rm Eur}\) comes entirely from the nodes where the
maximum selects \(g\): the terminal payoff is unchanged, but the holder can
take the intrinsic value instead of being forced to carry the position to
maturity.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{The hedge}

At a node \((n,S_n)\), the one-period replicating portfolio for the two
next-date option values uses
\[
\Delta_n=\frac{V_{n+1}^u-V_{n+1}^d}{(u-d)S_n},\qquad
B_n=\frac{uV_{n+1}^d-dV_{n+1}^u}{R(u-d)},\qquad
\Delta_nS_n+B_n=C_n.
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item If continuation is optimal, \(V_n=C_n\) and the portfolio delivers
      \(V_{n+1}\) in both states.
\item If exercise is optimal, \(V_n=g(S_n)\) and the seller's wealth
      finances the exercise payment.
\item If the holder continues when exercise was optimal, the seller holds
      \(V_n\geq C_n\), invests \(C_n\) in the hedge and keeps the surplus.
\end{itemize}
Hence tracking \(V\) superhedges every admissible exercise time, at cost
\(V_0\); no cheaper strategy survives every stopping choice of the buyer.

\subsection{Snell envelope}

\begin{definition}[Discounted Snell envelope]
With \(Z_n=R^{-n}Y_n\), define
\[
U_n=\esssup_{\tau\in\mathcal T_{n,N}}\E^\Q[Z_\tau\mid\cF_n].
\]
Then \(V_n=R^nU_n\), and the recursion reads
\(U_N=Z_N\), \(U_n=\max\{Z_n,\E^\Q[U_{n+1}\mid\cF_n]\}\).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
\(U\) is the smallest \(\Q\)-supermartingale dominating \(Z\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
From the recursion, \(U_n\geq Z_n\) and \(U_n\geq\E^\Q[U_{n+1}\mid\cF_n]\):
\(U\) is a supermartingale dominating \(Z\). If \(W\) is another such
supermartingale, then \(W_N\geq Z_N=U_N\), and if \(W_{n+1}\geq U_{n+1}\),
\(W_n\geq\max\{Z_n,\E^\Q[W_{n+1}\mid\cF_n]\}\geq U_n\). Backward induction
gives \(W\geq U\).
\end{proof}

Recall that an adapted, integrable process \(X\) is a \(\Q\)-supermartingale
if \(\E^\Q[X_u\mid\cF_t]\leq X_t\) for \(t\leq u\): it decreases on
average. The Snell envelope is the American analogue of risk-neutral
valuation: the minimal discounted process that can always cover an exercise
request.

\begin{keyidea}{Optimal policy}
One optimal stopping rule is the first entry into the exercise region:
\[
\tau_n^\star=\inf\{k\in\{n,\ldots,N\}:V_k=Y_k\}.
\]
Up to \(\tau_n^\star\), the discounted value \(U\) is a martingale: the option
is hedged like a European claim until it is exercised.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{studentexercise}{in the money is not enough}
At a node, an American put is in the money. Is immediate exercise optimal?
What must be compared, and what is the option value at the node?
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
Not necessarily. Compare the intrinsic value \(g(S_n)\) with the discounted
risk-neutral continuation value \(C_n\). The option is worth
\(\max\{g(S_n),C_n\}\); exercising when \(C_n>g(S_n)\) throws away
\(C_n-g(S_n)\).
\end{shortsolution}

\section{Continuous time: the obstacle problem}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Black--Scholes formulation}

Under \(\Q\), suppose
\[
\frac{\dd S_t}{S_t}=(r-q)\dd t+\sigma\dd W_t^\Q,
\qquad\sigma>0,
\]
with a continuous dividend yield \(q\geq0\) (\(q=0\) unless stated).

\paragraph{The notation \(\E^\Q_{t,s}\).}
This is expectation under \(\Q\) for the stock started at time \(t\)
with \(S_t=s\). For a future payoff \(h(S_T)\),
\[
\E^\Q_{t,s}[h(S_T)]=\E^\Q[h(S_T)\mid S_t=s].
\]
The Black--Scholes stock is Markov: its current value summarizes the past
information needed to predict its future. Thus, writing
\(F(t,s)=\E^\Q_{t,s}[h(S_T)]\),
\[
\E^\Q[h(S_T)\mid\cF_t]=F(t,S_t).
\]
For example, \(\E^\Q_{t,s}[S_T]=s e^{(r-q)(T-t)}\). With no dividends,
\(\E^\Q_{t,s}[e^{-r(T-t)}S_T]=s\).

\paragraph{The value function.}
Fix a stopping time \(\tau\). Receiving \(g(S_\tau)\) at \(\tau\) is a claim
whose no-arbitrage price is \(\E^\Q[e^{-r(\tau-t)}g(S_\tau)\mid\cF_t]\)
(Lectures~6--8). The holder picks the best \(\tau\), so the American value is
\[
\boxed{
v(t,s)=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal T_{t,T}}
\E^\Q_{t,s}[e^{-r(\tau-t)}g(S_\tau)].}
\]
Only the \(\Q\)-dynamics of \(S\) matter: drift \(r-q\), not \(\mu\). The
\emph{continuation region} is \(\{v>g\}\) and the \emph{exercise region}
is \(\{v=g\}\).

\subsection{Two inequalities and one equality}

Define the pricing operator, including discounting,
\[
\mathcal Af=(r-q)s\,f_s+\tfrac12\sigma^2s^2f_{ss}-rf,
\]
and assume, formally, that \(v\) is smooth enough for It\^o's formula. Then
\[
\dd\bigl(e^{-ru}v(u,S_u)\bigr)
=e^{-ru}(v_t+\mathcal Av)(u,S_u)\dd u
+e^{-ru}\sigma S_uv_s(u,S_u)\dd W_u^\Q,
\]
and taking expectations over a short interval \([t,t+\dd t]\),
\[
\E^\Q_{t,s}\!\left[e^{-r\dd t}v(t+\dd t,S_{t+\dd t})\right]
=v(t,s)+(v_t+\mathcal Av)(t,s)\dd t+o(\dd t).
\]
At \((t,s)\), the holder can either exercise now or wait \(\dd t\) and then
act optimally. Each choice gives an inequality; the optimal one gives an
equality.

\paragraph{Option 1: exercise now.}
Stopping at \(\tau=t\) is admissible and pays \(g(s)\), so
\[
\boxed{v(t,s)\geq g(s)\quad\text{for every }(t,s).}
\]
In a market, a put trading below \(K-s\) could be bought and exercised
immediately: an arbitrage.

\paragraph{Option 2: wait \(\dd t\).}
Waiting, then acting optimally, is always admissible:
\[
v(t,s)\geq\E^\Q_{t,s}\!\left[e^{-r\dd t}v(t+\dd t,S_{t+\dd t})\right]
=v(t,s)+(v_t+\mathcal Av)(t,s)\dd t+o(\dd t).
\]
Subtracting \(v(t,s)\), dividing by \(\dd t\) and letting \(\dd t\downarrow0\),
\[
\boxed{v_t+\mathcal Av\leq0\quad\text{everywhere, including the exercise region.}}
\]
Equivalently, \(e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)\) has nonpositive drift: it is a
\(\Q\)-supermartingale, the continuous-time Snell envelope.

\paragraph{In the continuation region, waiting is optimal.}
If \(v(t,s)>g(s)\), continuity of \(v-g\) and of the path imply that, for
small \(\dd t\), the path stays where \(v>g\); stopping in
\([t,t+\dd t]\) is never optimal. Option 2 then attains the maximum, so
\[
\boxed{v_t+\mathcal Av=0\quad\text{where }v>g.}
\]
This is the Black--Scholes PDE: until exercise, \(e^{-rt}v(t,S_t)\) is a
martingale, and the option is hedged like a European one.

\begin{keyidea}{The obstacle problem (variational inequality)}
\[
\boxed{
\max\{g-v,\;v_t+\mathcal Av\}=0,\qquad v(T,s)=g(s).}
\]
Equivalently, wherever the derivatives exist,
\[
v-g\geq0,\qquad -(v_t+\mathcal Av)\geq0,\qquad
(v-g)(v_t+\mathcal Av)=0.
\]
In the continuation region the PDE holds with equality. In the exercise
region \(v=g\) and only the inequality \(g_t+\mathcal Ag\leq0\) is required.
Unlike the European case, the region where the PDE holds is not known in
advance: finding it is part of the pricing problem.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{boardexample}{why waiting is costly deep in the money}
For a put with \(q=0\) and \(s<K\), \(g(s)=K-s\) and
\[
g_t+\mathcal Ag=rs(-1)-r(K-s)=-rK<0.
\]
In the exercise region, waiting an instant loses the interest \(rK\dd t\) on
the strike, which is why the inequality is strict there.
\end{boardexample}

\begin{warningbox}{A formal argument}
At the exercise boundary, \(v\) is only \(C^1\) in \(s\), not \(C^2\), so
It\^o's formula does not apply directly. A rigorous proof uses the Snell
envelope in continuous time or viscosity solutions.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{A non-dividend-paying call}
\label{sec:calls}

\begin{proposition}[No early exercise for a standard call]
If \(q=0\) and \(r\geq0\), then
\[
C^{\rm Am}(t,s)=C^{\rm Eur}(t,s)
\]
for a call payoff \((s-K)^+\). Early exercise is never strictly optimal
before maturity.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For \(\theta=T-t>0\),
\[
C^{\rm Eur}(t,s)
\geq e^{-r\theta}\E^\Q_{t,s}[S_T-K]
=s-Ke^{-r\theta}\geq s-K.
\]
Together with nonnegativity, this gives
\(C^{\rm Eur}(t,s)\geq(s-K)^+\). At any stopping time, retaining the
European call is at least as valuable as exercising it, and its discounted
price is a martingale.
\end{proof}

\begin{warningbox}{The dividend qualification matters}
When \(q>0\), or with a discrete dividend, exercising before an
ex-dividend date gives ownership of the stock and therefore the dividend.
An American call may then be exercised early. Never quote the preceding
result without the no-dividend assumption.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{studentexercise}{what do dividends change?}
Which object changes with dividends: the payoff, the stopping-time logic, or
the continuation value?
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
The payoff and the stopping-time logic are unchanged. Dividends alter the
economics of waiting, hence the continuation value and the exercise boundary.
\end{shortsolution}

\subsection{The American put: exercise boundary}

From now on, \(q=0\), \(r\geq0\) and \(g(s)=(K-s)^+\). There is no
closed-form price: both the value \(v\) and the exercise boundary must be
computed numerically. The obstacle problem is completed by
\[
v(T,s)=(K-s)^+,\qquad v(t,0)=K,\qquad
\lim_{s\to\infty}v(t,s)=0.
\]
At zero stock price, immediate exercise pays \(K\); postponing that payment
cannot improve its discounted value when \(r\geq0\). On a numerical domain
\([0,S_{\max}]\), with \(S_{\max}>K\), replace the condition at infinity
by \(v(t,S_{\max})\approx0\), and check the truncation error by increasing
\(S_{\max}\).

In the standard model with \(r>0\), there is a boundary \(s^*(t)<K\) such that
\[
\mathcal E=\{(t,s):s\leq s^*(t)\}\ \text{(exercise)},\qquad
\mathcal C=\{(t,s):s>s^*(t)\}\ \text{(continue)}.
\]
Deep below \(K\), waiting delays cash while adding little useful
optionality; near the strike, continuation is valuable. The boundary
increases with \(t\) and approaches \(K\) from below as maturity approaches.
At the boundary, the value joins the payoff smoothly (value matching and
smooth fit):
\[
\boxed{v(t,s^*(t))=K-s^*(t),\qquad v_s(t,s^*(t))=-1.}
\]
The boundary is free: it is a result of the pricing problem, not a contract
input.

\begin{keyidea}{The boundary is the decision rule}
The optimal exercise time is the first touch of the boundary,
\[
\tau^\star=\inf\{u\geq t:S_u\leq s^*(u)\}\wedge T,
\]
which uses only the path so far: it is a stopping time. A path that never
touches \(s^*\) is held to maturity. Being deep in the money is not enough:
the path must cross \(s^*(t)\), which may lie well below \(K\). The price is
\(v(0,S_0)=\E^\Q[e^{-r\tau^\star}(K-S_{\tau^\star})^+]\).
\end{keyidea}

\begin{studentexercise}{identify the region}
For an American put, a numerical scheme reports at some node an intrinsic
value strictly below the continuation estimate. Which region contains the
node and what is the option value there?
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
It is in the continuation region and the value is the continuation estimate.
Intrinsic value is a lower obstacle, not necessarily the price.
\end{shortsolution}

\section{Computing American prices}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Three numerical routes}

Pricing the American put means solving its obstacle problem. Three methods
are standard; they approximate different objects.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.17\textwidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.38\textwidth}}
\toprule
Method & What is approximated & How the continuation value is obtained\\
\midrule
Binomial tree
  & The \emph{stock model}: \(S\) moves to \(uS\) or \(dS\) at dates
    \(0,\Delta t,\ldots,T\)
  & Exactly, from the two children of each node\\
Longstaff--Schwartz
  & Only the \emph{exercise dates}: Black--Scholes paths are simulated;
    exercise is allowed at \(t_0,\ldots,t_N\)
  & Estimated, by regressing realized cash flows on functions of the
    current state across simulated paths\\
Finite differences
  & The \emph{PDE}: the obstacle problem on an \((s,t)\) grid
  & Through the discretized PDE operator, then the obstacle \(V\geq g\)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All three compare exercise with continuation. Convergence requires refining
the exercise grid and controlling each method's space, sampling, or regression errors.

\begin{warningbox}{An exercise grid is an approximation to continuous exercise}
For exercise restricted to \(0,h,2h,\ldots,T\), the Bermudan value satisfies
\[
v_h(t,s)=\max\{g(s),\E^\Q_{t,s}[e^{-rh}v_h(t+h,S_{t+h})]\}.
\]
For a finite \(h\), this forbids exercise between consecutive grid dates.
The continuous-time American value is recovered as the mesh tends to zero;
the same finite-step identity is not exact for unrestricted exercise.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{The binomial model: backward induction on a tree}

With \(N\) steps of length \(\Delta t=T/N\), the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein
parameters are
\[
u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\qquad d=u^{-1},\qquad
p=\frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}.
\]
Node \((n,j)\) carries \(S_n^j=S_0u^jd^{n-j}\). At every node,
\[
\boxed{V_n^j=\max\left\{g(S_n^j),\ e^{-r\Delta t}
\left[pV_{n+1}^{j+1}+(1-p)V_{n+1}^j\right]\right\}.}
\]
The tree supplies both a price and a discrete approximation to the exercise
boundary: mark every node at which the maximum selects \(g(S_n^j)\). The
tree recombines, so it has \(O(N^2)\) nodes.

\subsection{Simulation: the Longstaff--Schwartz method}

\paragraph{Simulation uses the Black--Scholes model, not a tree.}
Monte Carlo draws \(M\) paths directly from the Black--Scholes
\(\Q\)-dynamics, exactly:
\[
S_{t_{k+1}}=S_{t_k}\exp\Bigl(\bigl(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)\Delta t
+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_{k+1}\Bigr),\qquad Z_{k+1}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)
\text{ i.i.d.}
\]
Nothing is binomial: the stock can take any positive value, and paths do not
recombine, so each simulated state is visited once. The only discrete feature
is the set of exercise dates \(0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=T\): Longstaff--Schwartz
prices a \emph{Bermudan} option, which approaches the American price as the
exercise grid is refined.

\paragraph{Why plain Monte Carlo is not enough.}
For a European option, simulation estimates \(e^{-rT}\E^\Q[g(S_T)]\) by
averaging terminal discounted payoffs. An American decision at an
intermediate state requires the conditional continuation value
\(\E^\Q[\text{future discounted cash flow}\mid S_{t_k}=s]\). In a tree, this
is an average over the two children of the node. A simulated state has no
siblings to average over, so the function must be \emph{estimated}.

\begin{keyidea}{Key idea: a cross-sectional regression}
With \(\tau\) the optimal exercise date after \(t_k\), the continuation value
is a function of the current state:
\[
C_k(s)=\E^\Q\!\left[e^{-r(\tau-t_k)}g(S_\tau)\mid S_{t_k}=s\right]
\approx\sum_{\ell=0}^L\beta_{k,\ell}\,\psi_\ell(s),
\]
for basis functions \(\psi_0,\ldots,\psi_L\) (e.g.\ \(1,s,s^2\), or Laguerre
polynomials). The coefficients are fitted \emph{across paths}: each path
gives one point \(\bigl(S^{(m)}_{t_k},\text{its realized discounted cash
flow}\bigr)\).
\end{keyidea}

\paragraph{What does conditioning mean numerically?}
Least-squares Monte Carlo (LSMC) approximates a conditional mean by regression.
Fix one exercise date \(t_k\) and a later stopping rule \(\tau>t_k\).
For this paragraph, treat that rule as fixed, and define
\[
X=S_{t_k},\qquad Y=e^{-r(\tau-t_k)}g(S_\tau),\qquad
c(s)=\E^\Q[Y\mid X=s].
\]
Each simulated path supplies a pair \((X^{(m)},Y^{(m)})\): the current
stock price and a noisy observation of the later discounted cash flow.
The ordinary average \(M^{-1}\sum_mY^{(m)}\) estimates \(\E^\Q[Y]\), a
single number that pools different current states. The exercise decision
needs a function of the state, \(c(s)\). For a continuously distributed
stock price, exact repetitions of \(X=s\) almost surely do not occur in a
finite sample. Conditioning therefore cannot be implemented by simply
averaging the paths with exactly that stock price. Regression pools
information across the cloud of pairs by fitting a common function.

If \(\tau\) is the optimal later rule, \(c=C_k\), the true continuation
function. In the backward algorithm, the optimal rule is unknown: the
response \(Y\) uses the later rule learned so far. The fit estimates the
continuation value of that rule, which is then improved by allowing
exercise at \(t_k\).

\begin{keyidea}{Conditional expectation is an orthogonal projection}
Suppose \(\E^\Q[Y^2]<\infty\). Write
\(Y=c(X)+\varepsilon\), where \(\E^\Q[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\).
For every square-integrable function \(f(X)\),
\[
\boxed{\E^\Q[(Y-f(X))^2]
=\E^\Q[(Y-c(X))^2]+\E^\Q[(c(X)-f(X))^2].}
\]
Indeed, the cross term vanishes by conditioning on \(X\):
\[
\E^\Q[(Y-c(X))(c(X)-f(X))]
=\E^\Q[(c(X)-f(X))\E^\Q[\varepsilon\mid X]]=0.
\]
Thus \(c\) minimizes the population mean squared prediction error over all
functions of the current state. The first term is future uncertainty;
only the second term can be reduced by improving the mean function.
\end{keyidea}

\paragraph{Two approximations turn this identity into LSMC.}
First restrict the candidate functions to a finite span:
\[
f_\beta(s)=\sum_{\ell=0}^L\beta_\ell\psi_\ell(s),\qquad
\beta^*=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\beta}
\E^\Q\!\left[\left(Y-f_\beta(X)\right)^2\right].
\]
For example, \(\psi_0=1\), \(\psi_1(s)=s\), \(\psi_2(s)=s^2\) give a
quadratic approximation. Second replace the expectation by the empirical
average over the selected paths. If \(n_k=|I_k|\), define the design matrix
\(\Psi\in\R^{n_k\times(L+1)}\) and response vector \(y\) by
\[
\Psi_{m\ell}=\psi_\ell(S^{(m)}_{t_k}),\qquad
y_m=e^{-r(\tau^{(m)}-t_k)}CF^{(m)},\qquad m\in I_k.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\widehat\beta_k=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\beta}\|y-\Psi\beta\|_2^2,
\qquad \widehat C_k(s)=\sum_{\ell=0}^L\widehat\beta_{k,\ell}\psi_\ell(s).}
\]
The normal equations are
\(\Psi^\top\Psi\widehat\beta_k=\Psi^\top y\). If \(\Psi\) has full
column rank, the solution is unique; the familiar inverse formula is an
algebraic description, not a recommended numerical implementation.
Use a QR or SVD least-squares solver. There is one row per path and one
column per basis function, and a separate fit at each date.

When the fit uses only in-the-money paths, the population objective is
conditioned on \(g(X)>0\). This changes the region weighted by the fit,
but not \(\E^\Q[Y\mid X=s]\) at an in-the-money state \(s\), because
selection depends only on \(X\).

\begin{warningbox}{Sampling error, basis error, and the information set}
For a fixed basis and an appropriate sampling law, more paths stabilize the
least-squares coefficients around the population projection. They do not
remove the error from an insufficient basis. Increasing the basis dimension
with too few paths can instead overfit the simulated future outcomes.
Scale the state, for example by using \(S/K\), and use substantially more
in-the-money observations than basis functions. If there are no such paths,
skip the fit; if the design is rank deficient, reduce the basis or use an
explicitly chosen fallback.

The regressors must contain the state needed to predict continuation using
current information. For a vanilla Black--Scholes put, \(S_{t_k}\) suffices.
For an Asian payoff or stochastic volatility, include the running average
or volatility as well. Future stock prices must never be regressors.
Refining the exercise grid alone does not remove sampling or basis errors.
\end{warningbox}

\paragraph{The algorithm.}
Each path \(m\) carries a cash flow \(CF^{(m)}\) and its date
\(\tau^{(m)}\) under the current exercise rule.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Simulate} \(M\) independent paths
      \(S^{(m)}_{t_0},\ldots,S^{(m)}_{t_N}\) under \(\Q\).
\item \textbf{Initialize at maturity.} For every path,
      \(CF^{(m)}=g(S^{(m)}_T)\) and \(\tau^{(m)}=T\).
\item \textbf{Backward loop.} For \(k=N-1,N-2,\ldots,1\):
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] \emph{Select} the in-the-money paths
        \(I_k=\{m:g(S^{(m)}_{t_k})>0\}\).
  \item[(b)] For \(m\in I_k\), compute the realized discounted cash flow
        \(Y^{(m)}=e^{-r(\tau^{(m)}-t_k)}CF^{(m)}\).
  \item[(c)] \emph{Regress} by least squares:
        \[
        \widehat\beta_k=\arg\min_\beta\sum_{m\in I_k}
        \Bigl(Y^{(m)}-\sum_{\ell=0}^L\beta_\ell\,\psi_\ell(S^{(m)}_{t_k})\Bigr)^2,
        \qquad
        \widehat C_k(s)=\sum_{\ell}\widehat\beta_{k,\ell}\,\psi_\ell(s).
        \]
  \item[(d)] \emph{Decide and update:} for \(m\in I_k\), if
        \(g(S^{(m)}_{t_k})>\widehat C_k(S^{(m)}_{t_k})\), set
        \(CF^{(m)}\leftarrow g(S^{(m)}_{t_k})\) and
        \(\tau^{(m)}\leftarrow t_k\). Otherwise keep the later cash flow.
  \end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Price at \(t_0\).} All paths share \(S_0\), so no regression
      is needed:
      \[
      \boxed{\widehat V_0=\max\Bigl\{g(S_0),\ \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^M
      e^{-r\tau^{(m)}}CF^{(m)}\Bigr\}.}
      \]
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Worked calculation: the regression at \(t=2\).}
Use the eight stock paths in the slides, with \(K=1.10\), \(r=0.06\),
dates \(0,1,2,3\), and basis \(1,s,s^2\). Before the first backward step,
all cash flows are terminal put payoffs. At \(t=2\), paths \(1,3,4,6,7\)
are in the money. In that order, the numerical least-squares problem is
\[
\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}
1&1.08&1.1664\\
1&1.07&1.1449\\
1&0.97&0.9409\\
1&0.77&0.5929\\
1&0.84&0.7056
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
y=e^{-0.06}\begin{pmatrix}0\\0.07\\0.18\\0.20\\0.09\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Solving gives
\[
\widehat C_2(s)=-1.069988+2.983411s-1.813576s^2
\quad\text{(coefficients rounded)}.
\]
The response is already valued at \(t=2\), so no extra discount factor is
applied when comparing the fitted value with immediate exercise.

\begin{tabular}{c|rrrrl}
\toprule
Path & \(S_2\) & Realized \(Y\) & Fitted \(\widehat C_2\) & \(g(S_2)\) & Decision\\
\midrule
1 & 1.08 & 0.0000 & 0.0367 & 0.0200 & continue\\
3 & 1.07 & 0.0659 & 0.0459 & 0.0300 & continue\\
4 & 0.97 & 0.1695 & 0.1175 & 0.1300 & exercise\\
6 & 0.77 & 0.1884 & 0.1520 & 0.3300 & exercise\\
7 & 0.84 & 0.0848 & 0.1564 & 0.2600 & exercise\\

\end{tabular}

For example, path 4 exercises even though its eventual discounted terminal
payoff happens to exceed \(0.13\): the decision compares \(0.13\) with
the fitted conditional mean \(0.1175\), not with that future realization.

\paragraph{Move backward: the response changes when the rule changes.}
After this step, paths \(4,6,7\) carry cash flows \(0.13,0.33,0.26\)
at date \(2\). At \(t=1\), the in-the-money paths are \(1,4,6,7,8\).
In this order, their current-price and response vectors are
\[
\begin{aligned}
x&=(1.09,\ 0.93,\ 0.76,\ 0.92,\ 0.88)^\top,\\
y&=e^{-0.06}(0,\ 0.13,\ 0.33,\ 0.26,\ 0)^\top.
\end{aligned}
\]
These are cash flows under the updated later rule; regressing the original
terminal payoffs again would ignore the decisions just learned. The fit is
\[
\widehat C_1(s)=2.037512-3.335443s+1.356457s^2.
\]
Path 1 continues; paths \(4,6,7,8\) exercise at \(t=1\). The final
nonzero cash flows are \(0.17,0.34,0.18,0.22\) at \(t=1\) and
\(0.07\) on path 3 at \(t=3\). Average over \emph{all eight paths},
including the zero payments:
\[
\widehat V_0=\max\left\{0.10,\frac18\left[
(0.17+0.34+0.18+0.22)e^{-0.06}+0.07e^{-0.18}\right]\right\}
\approx0.1144.
\]
This is an in-sample illustration, not an independent accuracy check.

\begin{studentexercise}{realized future or conditional mean?}
For path 1 at \(t=2\), \(S_2=1.08\), \(Y=0\), and
\(\widehat C_2(1.08)=0.0367\). Should the fitted rule exercise the put?
Why would using \(Y\) directly give a different decision?
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
Continue, because \(g(1.08)=0.02<0.0367\). Comparing with \(Y=0\)
would instead say exercise, but uses a future outcome unavailable at the
decision time. The regression estimates a conditional mean; it does not
reveal the future cash flow of an individual path.
\end{shortsolution}

\paragraph{Why each design choice.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Regress realized cash flows, not estimated values.} \(Y^{(m)}\) is
      what the path actually pays under the current rule. The regression only
      \emph{selects} the rule; the price averages real cash flows, so
      regression errors affect the exercise policy and hence the cash-flow
      targets used at earlier dates. Keeping realized payments avoids
      substituting fitted values for the cash flows being priced.
\item \emph{Use only in-the-money paths.} Out of the money, \(g=0\) and
      exercise is never chosen. Fitting only where the decision matters
      improves the fit with few basis functions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warningbox}{A policy value is a lower bound in expectation}
Freeze the learned rule \(\widehat\tau\), including the decision at
\(t_0\), using training data. On independent valuation paths, the average
of \(e^{-r\widehat\tau}g(S_{\widehat\tau})\) is unbiased for that fixed
policy's value, which is at most the optimal Bermudan value. A particular
Monte Carlo average can exceed the optimum through sampling noise; the
lower-bound statement concerns the policy's expectation. Choosing again
between exercise at \(t_0\) and continuation using the valuation-sample
maximum would add selection bias, so freeze that choice as well.

Reusing training paths for valuation can create upward foresight bias,
which need not be small. Fit on one set of paths and evaluate the frozen
rule on an independent set, reporting the Monte Carlo standard error.
Accuracy also depends on the basis and exercise dates; duality methods
(Andersen--Broadie) can complement the policy value with an upper bound.
\end{warningbox}

\subsection{Finite differences: the explicit scheme}

Keep the no-dividend American put assumptions. Choose
\[
s_i=i\Delta s,\quad \Delta s=S_{\max}/M,\qquad
t_n=n\Delta t,\quad \Delta t=T/N,
\]
and write \(V_i^n\approx v(t_n,s_i)\), \(g_i=(K-s_i)^+\).
Initialize \(V_i^N=g_i\), fix \(V_0^n=K\), \(V_M^n=0\), and march
backward from \(n=N-1\) to \(0\). At an interior stock node, use centered
differences
\[
v_s(t_n,s_i)\approx\frac{V_{i+1}^n-V_{i-1}^n}{2\Delta s},\qquad
v_{ss}(t_n,s_i)\approx
\frac{V_{i+1}^n-2V_i^n+V_{i-1}^n}{(\Delta s)^2}.
\]
Let \(\mathcal A_h\) be the resulting discrete pricing operator.
An explicit continuation step evaluates it at the already known level:
\[
\frac{V_i^{n+1}-C_i^n}{\Delta t}
+(\mathcal A_hV^{n+1})_i=0.
\]
Since \(s_i=i\Delta s\), this gives the update
\[
\boxed{
C_i^n=a_iV_{i-1}^{n+1}+b_iV_i^{n+1}+c_iV_{i+1}^{n+1},\qquad
V_i^n=\max\{g_i,C_i^n\},}
\]
where
\[
a_i=\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\sigma^2i^2-ri),\qquad
b_i=1-\Delta t(\sigma^2i^2+r),\qquad
c_i=\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\sigma^2i^2+ri).
\]
The weights sum to \(1-r\Delta t\), the first-order approximation to the
one-step discount factor.

\begin{warningbox}{Positivity and the explicit time-step restriction}
Require \(a_i,b_i,c_i\geq0\) for all \(1\leq i\leq M-1\). With
\(r\geq0\), sufficient conditions for the centered stencil are
\[
\sigma^2i^2\geq ri\quad\text{at every interior node},\qquad
\Delta t\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^2(M-1)^2+r}.
\]
Then the continuation update is monotone and its nonnegative weights sum
to at most one; taking the maximum with the payoff preserves monotonicity.
Reducing \(\Delta t\) fixes a negative \(b_i\), but cannot fix a negative
\(a_i\). If the drift dominates diffusion near zero, replace the centered
drift there by a monotone one-sided approximation and recompute the weights.
For \(r\geq0\), the forward difference
\(rs_i(V_{i+1}-V_i)/\Delta s\) gives nonnegative off-diagonal weights.
\end{warningbox}

\paragraph{The explicit algorithm, step by step.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Grid and check.} Choose \(S_{\max}\), \(M\), \(N\); set
      \(\Delta s=S_{\max}/M\), \(\Delta t=T/N\). Compute \(a_i,b_i,c_i\) for
      \(i=1,\ldots,M-1\) and check that all are nonnegative; otherwise
      increase \(N\) (or change the drift stencil if some \(a_i<0\)).
\item \textbf{Initialize at maturity.} For \(i=0,\ldots,M\):
      \(g_i=(K-s_i)^+\) and \(V_i^N=g_i\).
\item \textbf{Backward loop.} For \(n=N-1,N-2,\ldots,0\):
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] \emph{Boundaries:} \(V_0^n=K\), \(V_M^n=0\).
  \item[(b)] \emph{Continuation:} for \(i=1,\ldots,M-1\), using only the
        known level \(n+1\),
        \(C_i^n=a_iV_{i-1}^{n+1}+b_iV_i^{n+1}+c_iV_{i+1}^{n+1}\).
        Compute all interior nodes from the old array before replacing it.
  \item[(c)] \emph{Decide:} \(V_i^n=\max\{g_i,C_i^n\}\). If
        \(g_i\geq C_i^n\) and \(g_i>0\), flag node \((n,i)\) as
        \emph{exercise}.
  \end{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Output.} Interpolate \(V^0\) at \(s=S_0\) for the price. At
      each \(t_n\), the largest flagged \(s_i\) approximates \(s^*(t_n)\).
\end{enumerate}
Each step costs \(O(M)\) operations and needs no linear solve: the maximum is
applied node by node, exactly as on a binomial tree. The price of this
simplicity is the stability condition: \(\Delta t\) must shrink like
\((\Delta s)^2\), so \(N\) grows like \(M^2\).

\begin{remark}[What one step before maturity shows]
Where the payoff is locally linear, \(s_{i\pm1}<K\), the stencil applied
to \(g=K-s\) gives exactly \(C_i^{N-1}=g_i-rK\Delta t<g_i\): the PDE step
loses the interest on the strike, and the maximum restores \(g_i\), so the
node is in the exercise region. At the strike node, the kink gives
\(C_i^{N-1}=a_i\Delta s>0=g_i\): keeping the option has value. The PDE step and
the stopping decision are both visible in the first backward step.
\end{remark}

Method & Best fit & Main caution\\
\midrule
Binomial tree & One underlying; teaching and robust benchmarks
  & Many steps may be needed for convergence\\
Finite differences & Low-dimensional Markov models; boundary analysis

Longstaff--Schwartz & Several risk factors, or path simulation available
  & Quality depends on paths, basis, and regression design\\

\begin{warningbox}{Mandatory numerical checks}
Refine the time grid (and the space grid or number of paths) and verify
\[
V^{\rm Am}\geq V^{\rm Eur}\geq0,\qquad
V^{\rm Am,put}\geq(K-S_0)^+.
\]
For a non-dividend-paying call, the American method should converge to the
European benchmark. A material gap is a diagnostic warning.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{studentexercise}{diagnose two numerical errors}
You price an up-and-in American put by Monte Carlo with daily monitoring.
Name one issue caused by the barrier and one caused by the American feature.
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
A path may cross the barrier between monitoring dates, creating monitoring
bias; refine the grid or use a bridge correction. The American feature
requires a stopping policy, so test exercise dates, regression bases, and
out-of-sample policy performance.
\end{shortsolution}

\section{Synthesis and guided practice}
% ============================================================

\begin{keyidea}{A practical workflow}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the payoff and permitted exercise dates.
  \item Compare exercise and risk-neutral continuation values.
  \item In a tree, apply the Bellman maximum from maturity backward.
  \item With simulated paths, estimate continuation values by regression.
  \item In continuous time, solve the obstacle problem and locate stopping
        and continuation regions.
  \item Check numerical output against bounds and refinements.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{studentexercise}{final stopping-policy check}
At a node, an American put has immediate exercise value \(E\) and
continuation value \(C\), with \(E>C\). A colleague reports the option value
\(E+C\). Explain the error, state the correct value, and give the action.
\end{studentexercise}
\begin{shortsolution}
Exercise and continuation are mutually exclusive policies, so the recursion
takes their maximum: \(V=\max\{E,C\}=E\). The holder exercises. The
continuation value belongs to a different policy.
\end{shortsolution}

\begin{keyidea}{Final synthesis}
\[
\boxed{\text{American pricing}
=\text{no-arbitrage valuation}+\text{an optimal stopping decision}.}
\]
The Snell envelope describes optimal stopping; trees, LSMC, and finite differences compute it.
\end{keyidea}
